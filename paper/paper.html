From Culture-Level Plans to Verified Robot Motions: Reproducible and Auditable Experiments in a Robotic Laboratory Twin

[Author names and affiliations]

ILLUSTRATIVE: the experiment ran in a digital twin with a surrogate culture model; no biomedical parameter is asserted. This draft has not been submitted anywhere.

AbstractLaboratory automation promises reproducible experiments. Yet an automated protocol is usually written at the level of devices and robot programs, so the scientific intent, its laboratory realization and the record of what happened are hard to separate, to check and to reproduce. We describe a pipeline in which an experiment is stated once, as a plan over the cultures: what is done to them, how they are observed and what becomes known. Everything else is derived from it: the experimental design by expected information gain, a partially ordered plan of laboratory action designators, a structured reactive controller in CRAM/RPL, 151 grounded robot and device steps, and 111 constraint-based motion specifications for Giskard. A machine-checked refinement proof shows that, under six stated assumptions about controllers, sensors and devices, every execution of the motion plan either realizes the culture plan while satisfying all stated safety, timing and contamination constraints, or is stopped by a monitor and ends in a failure handler whose guarantee holds. The derivation is recorded in a content-addressed manifest. A complete re-run reproduced all 47 outputs of its 12 stages identically; only wall-clock timing fields are excluded from the comparison. Execution produces a hash-chained audit trail that is itself reproducible: re-executing each of the 12 symbolic scenarios yields the identical chain head. We demonstrate the pipeline on a challenge–rescue organoid experiment in a physics-based digital twin of a two-arm robotic cell. The biology is a surrogate model and all biomedical parameters are illustrative. The paper itself is a build product of the pipeline.

1Introduction

Robotic laboratories can run experiments without interruption and record every action they take [1, 2, 3, 4]. Reproducibility, however, needs more than automation [5]. A protocol for a liquid handler and a plate-moving robot fixes how an experiment is executed in one laboratory. It does not state what the experiment does to the biological material, and it cannot be checked against that intent. When the laboratory changes (another robot, another incubator, a new safety rule), the protocol has to be rewritten, and nothing shows that the new protocol performs the same experiment.

We take the opposite route. The experiment is written once, at the level of the cultures: which culture is challenged, rescued, grown, observed, interpreted and analysed; which physical change each action causes; and which knowledge it produces. The plan says nothing about robots, devices, poses, safety rules, cleanliness or temperature control. Everything below it is derived and checked: the design, the laboratory plan, the controller, the robot and device steps, and the robot motions. The derivation is recorded so that anyone with the inputs can reproduce it. The execution is recorded so that anyone can verify it and re-execute it.

Contributions:

  • A culture-level plan language with physical and knowledge effects, built on entity-based designators. A partial order is derived from causal links, and the plan is independent of the laboratory: we show this over 13 laboratory variants (Section 4).
  • An automatic refinement from the culture plan to a least-cost design by expected information gain, to laboratory action designators with contracts and failure handlers, to a CRAM/RPL structured reactive controller, and to Giskard motion statecharts (Section 5).
  • A machine-checked refinement proof that the motion plan carries out the culture plan and satisfies every stated constraint, or fails safely, relative to explicit assumptions (Section 6).
  • A reproducible derivation and a reproducible audit trail: a content-addressed manifest that a re-run reproduces, and hash-chained trails whose chain heads are reproduced by re-execution (Section 7).
  • The paper as a build product. Every number, table, figure and listing in this paper is generated from the pipeline's outputs (manifest a8a39851a44328aa).

2Related work

Robot scientists and self-driving laboratories. Adam and Eve closed the loop from hypothesis to experiment in yeast genetics and drug screening [1, 2]. A mobile robot ran a chemistry search over a week of autonomous experiments [3]. Self-driving laboratories are now common in chemistry and materials science [4]. These systems describe experiments at the level of the instruments. We add a level above the instruments and prove that the levels below realize it.

Plan languages for robots. RPL introduced concurrent reactive plans with failure handling and fluents [6]. Structured reactive controllers [7] and CRAM [8] made such plans the basis of everyday manipulation, using designators as symbolic descriptions that are resolved at run time, with knowledge supplied by KnowRob [9]. Giskard specifies motions as constraint- and optimization-based tasks solved by model-predictive control [10]. We use these layers unchanged and add a culture level above them, together with proofs across the levels.

Planning, design and verification. The partial order of our plans follows least-commitment planning: causal links and threats [11]. Designs are selected by expected information gain [12, 13], and the least-cost plan is found with answer set programming [14]. Proof obligations are discharged by an SMT solver [15]. The refinement argument follows refinement mappings [16] and assume-guarantee contracts [17].

Provenance and data integrity. The audit trail is a hash chain in the sense of [18]. Its entries follow W3C PROV [19] and the ALCOA+ expectations for regulated records [20]: attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original and accurate. The FAIR principles [21] motivate publishing the manifest together with the data.

3The experiment and the laboratory

The experiment asks which of three recovery models explains the efficacy of a rescue that follows a challenge: graded recovery (M1), a threshold (M2), or a protective effect (M3). Three cultures take part. The rescued culture is challenged at 0 h and rescued later. The comparator is challenged only. The control is untreated. All three are imaged at 72 h, and the organoid radius is measured from the images. Doses are in arbitrary units (au). The culture dynamics, the imaging and the models are Cell 01's culture surrogate. The numbers are ILLUSTRATIVE and are not claims about any biological system.

Cell 01 (Fig. 1) is a robotic cell for culture handling and liquid intervention. A seven-axis arm (R01) serves the incubator (L10) and the handover nest. A rail-mounted arm (R02) serves the liquid handler (L12), the imager (L17), a calibration and QC station (L21) and a quarantine station (H4), and changes tools at a rack. The cell is modelled as a semantic digital twin: an OWL ontology of the devices, seats, frames and safety rules, the component records of every device, and a USD scene that Isaac Sim simulates.

4The culture-level plan

A culture-level plan is a set of action designators over entity designators. An entity designator (det sort ?var slot*) describes an entity by its properties; it is resolved against a knowledge base and never names a device. Each action states its physical effect on a culture and its knowledge effect: what is recorded or inferred. Listing 1 shows the rescue. The goal of the plan is epistemic: the belief over the three models must have an expected information gain of at least 0.5 bit.

The order is not written. It is derived from causal links: an action's requirement must be supported by an earlier action's physical or knowledge effect, or by the given state. The 12 actions have 13 causal links, and the partial order is their transitive reduction (Fig. 2). Interpretation requires a known image scale. This requirement is marked establishable: the plan states it, and the laboratory must establish it.

Independence from the laboratory. We checked the plan text against the realization vocabularies of the knowledge base: robots, devices, poses, seats, tools, safety rules, cleanliness and temperature. No term occurs in the plan. We then ran the whole refinement in 13 laboratory variants that change robots, devices, the layout, safety constraints, cleanliness and temperature profiles (appendix, Table 9). The culture plan, its hash and its derived order are identical in all of them. Where a variant cannot realize the plan, the refusal names the missing capability. The plan itself is not changed.

5Refinement to robot motions

Design. The free parameters of the culture plan (challenge dose, rescue delay, replicates) are chosen by expected information gain over a uniform prior on M1–M3. The gain is estimated by nested Monte Carlo on the surrogate (800 samples, seed 7). Of 45 candidates, 13 reach 0.5 bit (Fig. 3). An answer set program over the laboratory facts then selects the least-cost safe plan among them: challenge 0.85 au, rescue 0.7 au after 48 h, 1 replicate, EIG 0.5439 bit, at a cost of 9986 decisecond-equivalents.

Laboratory plan. Each culture action is refined into a laboratory action designator (appendix, Table 7). A laboratory action states what it requires, what it prevents and maintains during a scope bounded by fluents, what it achieves, its goal, the failures it signals, and an instrument role: a slot that accepts the plate and a process that performs the action (Listing 2). Instruments are resolved by capability. A device fills the role if it has the slot and can run the process. Process models of the devices list their faults, the monitors that detect them and the handlers that repair them. The checker verifies the causal links, resolves all 9 threats, resolves every instrument and verifies the guarantee of each of the 13 handlers: goal, goal-or-safe-state, safe state or containment.

Controller. The laboratory plan is compiled into a CRAM/RPL structured reactive controller (Listing 3). The partial order becomes partial-order constraints. prevents becomes a with-policy over a monitored fluent, bounded by a scope. requires becomes ensure with the epistemic grade the contract demands. Handlers become with-failure-handling clauses with retry counters.

Steps. The kernel expands the controller into grounded steps: 151 robot motions and device commands in 3 trips. The answer set program derives the same plan independently, and both agree step for step and second for second. The z3 validator proves executability, the safety rules (L12 airspace, environment before storage, calibration before imaging, interlocks, identity), the 600 s window outside the incubator, the realization of every dose and image, and least cost (10 negative controls).

Motions. Each robot step is refined into a giskardpy motion statechart (Listing 4). Goal frames are taken from the composed scene: seat frames, approach poses and well frames re-parented to the plate. A Cartesian task specifies the goal, velocity limits and level-keeping are weighted constraints, and collision avoidance is a constraint set. The quadratic program minimizes velocity and jerk over the horizon plus the weighted slacks. All 25 goal frames are reachable by inverse kinematics with joint margin. Because level-keeping and collision terms are soft, the generator adds monitors that cancel the motion when tilt, collision distance or duration exceeds its bound. This makes every soft constraint hold-or-cancel.

6Correctness of the refinement

Theorem. Under assumptions A1–A6 (Table 3), every execution of the motion plan does one of two things. (i) It realizes the culture plan: every physical and knowledge effect, in the derived order, at 0, 48 and 72 h of culture time, with the control culture untreated, while interlocks, L12 airspace, identity, environment before storage, calibration before imaging, the 600 s window, contamination freedom, tool hygiene, plate level and collision distance all hold. (ii) Or a monitor stops it, and it ends in a failure handler whose guarantee holds.

The proof composes seven obligations along the refinement chain (Fig. 4, Table 2). The step-level validator proves which doses, images and session times every execution produces. O2 maps them through the vessel map (rescued → A01, comparator → A02, control → A05) and shows with z3 that the negation of each culture-level obligation is unsatisfiable. O6 is where the soft optimization meets the logic. Each motion's end monitor implies its step's postcondition under A3. Each motion's timeout is 1.2 times its estimate, which is exactly the duration bound that the step-level timing proof assumes. A held plate's tilt and every collision distance cancel the motion when violated. O7 closes the failure branch: a cancellation raises a failure that propagates to a handler whose guarantee O3 verified.

Negative controls. To show that the checks are not vacuous, each of 8 mutations must make an obligation fail: a swapped vessel map, a dosed control, a missing control image, a contaminating tool, a held motion without its tilt monitor, a timeout beyond the timing bound, a motion that does not refine its step, and the validator's own mutated plans. All are caught (Table 10).

7Reproducible derivation and audit

Manifest. The pipeline runs in 12 stages, from the design to the protocol (Table 4). For each stage the manifest records the command, the sha256 of the code it ran and the sha256 of every file it wrote. The inputs (culture plan, registry, ontology ABox, answer set program, scene, surrogate code, duration traces and the recorded physics runs) and the tool versions are recorded once. JSON outputs are hashed in canonical form without the wall-clock fields (ground_s, solve_s, validator_wall_s, wall_s). Nothing else is excluded. The manifest hash identifies the whole derivation: a8a39851a44328aac152ee46629decd9cddc691b1e8c2b835c422bba7cd32659.

Reproduction. A second complete run, which also recomputes the Monte Carlo estimates from scratch, produced the same manifest hash. All 47 of 47 outputs are identical, including the plan, the proofs, the controller, the generated Giskard module and all audit trails.

Audit trail. Every lifecycle transition of every designator (open, grounded, accepted, executing, executed, failed, repaired) is one entry of a SHA-256 hash chain. The entry hash covers the previous hash and the canonical JSON of the entry (Listing 5). The executor writes the entries, not the plan, so a plan cannot skip them. Entries record the agent, the software (kernel, knowledge base, registry hash, backend), the simulated time, bindings and resolutions with their grounds, constraint verdicts in two columns (realization and validity), monitors, and evidence with its own sha256.

Verification. For all 15 trails the chain is intact. Changing a single value is detected at the changed entry. Re-executing each of the 12 symbolic scenarios in a fresh directory reproduces its trail exactly, chain head included (12/12). A trail is therefore more than a tamper-evident record: it is a reproducible one, and its head hash commits to the whole execution. The 52 competency questions of the kernel are audit questions: what was done, why, how, under which constraints, with which evidence, and what would have happened if. All 52 are answered correctly from the trails alone.

8Execution

Table 5 lists the executions. On the symbolic backend, 12 scenarios inject knowledge errors, faults and workflow errors: lapsed calibration, a lost tip, a swapped plate identity, a slow tool changer, a swapped or incapable imager, an unbound rescue, a dropped control, contamination and an incomplete component record. Every scenario ends as specified before it was run. The ends are executed, executed after a local repair, escalated to a human, or refused before anything moved.

In Isaac Sim, the nominal run executes all three trips (Fig. 5). The physics-timed trips deviate from the planned durations by +2.3 %, -12.6 %, -19.1 %, which lies within the ±20 % that the timing proof assumes, and every trip stays inside the 600 s window. The identity-swap run detects the wrong plate at the incubator's barcode read, quarantines it and escalates, as specified. The lapsed-calibration run does not end as specified. The calibration repair worked: the imager refused, the plate was set aside, the station recalibrated and the measurement was re-planned. But the comparator well then failed the focus check twice, and the session escalated. The images after the reload show a lateral shift of about 93 µm that the seat sensors did not register. The kernel's refocus repair cannot change the outcome, because the imager autofocuses on every image anyway. We report this failure because it marks the boundary of the proof: plate misseating on the imager is one of the two partly detected faults (Section 9).

Knowledge. Executed on the surrogate, the analysis moves the belief from uniform to M1 0.68, M2 0.15, M3 0.17. The realized information gain is 0.357 bit, below the expected 0.544 bit. The plan's goal is an expected gain, which a single outcome need not reach. A sequential design conditions the next plan on this posterior.

9Limitations

  • No wet-lab execution. The experiment ran on a digital twin with a surrogate culture model. All biomedical parameters are ILLUSTRATIVE.
  • Relative correctness. The theorem holds relative to A1–A6. It does not prove progress: a motion may be cancelled rather than complete. 6 of the 27 fault instances in the process models are only partly detected (well misregistration at the liquid handler, plate misseating at the imager). An undetected occurrence lies outside both branches of the theorem.
  • Physics. Compliant insertion into the nests has no Giskard counterpart yet. The 2 mm capture and the 2° tilt bound are ILLUSTRATIVE. The generated Giskard module has been checked against the giskardpy sources but not executed, because no semantic digital twin world of Cell 01 is loaded into Giskard. Collisions of R02 are not checked offline.
  • Reproduction scope. The reproduction ran on the same machine with the same environment. The Isaac Sim runs are recorded inputs: their chains are verified, but they were not re-executed, and physics simulation on a GPU is not expected to be bit-reproducible.
  • References and authorship. The reference list was compiled without database access and must be checked before submission.

10Conclusion

An experiment can be stated at the level at which a scientist thinks about it, as a plan over cultures and knowledge, and still be carried out by robots under proofs that connect the two levels. The derivation and the execution can both be reproduced from content-addressed records. The proof is only as strong as its assumptions, and the experiment in this paper found where they are weakest: in how plates sit in their nests. The next steps are to execute the motion statecharts in Giskard against the semantic digital twin, to close the two detection gaps with monitors that make misseating observable, and to run the same culture plan in a wet laboratory.
